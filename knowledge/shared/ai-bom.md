## 1. What an AI BOM Is

### Definition

An **AI Bill of Materials** is a structured, version-controlled inventory of every component that contributes to the behavior of a deployed AI system. The artifact is operational documentation owned by the product organization that builds and runs the system. It is the AI-domain analog of the **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)** that became regulatory baseline for traditional software through 2021-2024 (NTIA Minimum Elements, Executive Order 14028 mandating SBOMs for federal software, SPDX + CycloneDX as the canonical specification standards).

The AI BOM extends SBOM discipline to the components that an SBOM was never designed to inventory: foundation and fine-tuned models, training datasets, evaluation datasets, prompts and prompt templates, retrieval corpora, third-party AI APIs and MCP servers, fine-tuning recipes, and deployment artifacts. SBOM tooling — CycloneDX in particular — added AI-specific extensions through 2024-2025 ("CycloneDX ML-BOM" and the broader ML-BOM working stream), but the practice is still maturing. Most product organizations through Q2 2026 either lack an AI BOM entirely, maintain a partial one that covers models but not training data or retrieval corpora, or treat the BOM as a static audit deliverable rather than a live operational artifact.

### Why an AI BOM became regulator-load-bearing in 2026

Through 2024-2025 the AI BOM was a recommended-best-practice artifact: NIST AI 600-1's MEASURE function called for provenance documentation; CycloneDX ML-BOM published format extensions; NTIA hinted at expanding SBOM scope to ML components. None of these were enforced. The 2026 inflection has three drivers:

1. **EU AI Act Article 11 enforcement landing 2026-08-02** (per Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 Article 113 staged enforcement schedule). Article 11 requires **technical documentation** for high-risk AI systems, with the specific elements enumerated in Annex IV §§1-9. Annex IV §2 ("description of the elements of the AI system and of the process for its development") and §3 ("detailed information on the monitoring, functioning and control") cannot be satisfied without the underlying inventory of model, training data, evaluation data, and runtime components. The AI BOM is the **upstream evidentiary substrate** that Article 11 + Annex IV documentation rests on. Q2-3.4 `eu-ai-act-annex-iv.md` documents the Annex IV taxonomy; this pack documents the BOM that feeds it.

2. **Eightfold AI class action filed 2026-01-20** (California state court, naming Microsoft and PayPal as customer-defendants). The class theory: an AI candidate-scoring tool's output may qualify as a **"consumer report" under FCRA 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(d)**, with the AI vendor potentially qualifying as a **"consumer reporting agency" under § 1681a(f)**. If the theory holds at any stage, regulators and plaintiff counsel in adverse-action proceedings will reach for the AI BOM **first** — it is the artifact that enumerates whether the in-scope candidate-scoring component exists, what models it uses, what training data shaped it, and whether the producing entity meets the statutory definitions. Per `sensitive-skill-guardrails.md` §1.4 and §3.5.1, the four FCRA procedural obligations (pre-adverse-action notice, copy-of-report delivery, summary-of-rights, 30-day dispute window) attach to any adverse employment action; the BOM is the artifact that determines whether they were triggered. The class action is at pleading stage and the theory is novel and untested, but the exposure is live.

3. **State-level AI-disclosure regimes maturing**. **Texas TRAIGA** (Texas Responsible Artificial Intelligence Governance Act) imposes disclosure obligations on AI systems used in consequential decisions. **NYC Local Law 144 (AEDT)** requires bias audits and notice for automated employment decision tools. **California ICRAA + CCRAA** layer state-level investigative consumer reporting obligations on top of FCRA. **Illinois AI Video Interview Act** requires consent + notice. **Colorado AI Act (effective 2026)** requires impact assessments. Each regime reads from a different angle on the same underlying inventory: regulators and state Attorneys General use the AI BOM to establish what is in-scope. Q2-1.C `state-ai-hiring-laws.md` documents the regime-specific obligations; this pack documents the BOM contents that feed them.

## 2. The 8 AI BOM Components

Each component carries five fields: **What is inventoried**, **Why it matters**, **Required disclosure fields**, **Owner**, **Evidentiary use**.

### 2.1 Models — `[Owner: 💻]`

**What is inventoried**: Every foundation model, fine-tuned model, and embedded ML model that contributes to the system's behavior at runtime. Includes models invoked via API (Claude, GPT, Gemini, Bedrock, Vertex, Azure OpenAI), self-hosted open-weight models (Llama, Mistral, Qwen), fine-tuned variants the organization produced, and embedded classifiers (sentiment, intent, safety classifiers, retrieval rerankers).

**Why it matters**: The model is the locus of capability and the locus of risk. EU AI Act conformity assessments establish whether the system meets risk-management obligations at the model boundary. FCRA exposure attaches to the model that produces the candidate score. State AG investigations subpoena the model identity to establish in-scope-ness.

**Required disclosure fields per model**:
- Provider (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, internal, open-source maintainer)
- Model identifier and version (e.g., `claude-opus-4-7`, `gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09`)
- Training data cutoff date (provider-published or fine-tuning cutoff)
- License (proprietary terms, Apache-2.0, Llama Community License, etc.)
- Capabilities used (text generation, classification, embedding, vision, audio, tool use)
- Risk classification under EU AI Act (high-risk / limited-risk / minimal-risk per Article 6 + Annex III determination from Q2-1.A)
- Whether the model is a "GPAI" under Article 3(63) (relevant for upstream provider obligations)
- Fine-tuning lineage (if fine-tuned, point to recipe in §2.7)

**Owner**: `ai-architect` for model selection and lineage; `cio` for license posture and procurement; `compliance-officer` for risk-classification mapping.

**Evidentiary use**: Article 11 + Annex IV §1 (general description). FCRA proceedings establishing model identity. State AG investigations.

### 2.2 Training Data — `[Owner: 🛡️]`

**What is inventoried**: Every dataset used to pre-train (rare for product orgs), fine-tune, or instruct-tune any model in §2.1. Includes proprietary datasets, licensed datasets, web-scraped corpora, synthetic data, customer-derived data, and reinforcement-learning-from-human-feedback (RLHF) datasets.

**Why it matters**: Training data is the most opaque and most contentious component of an AI system. It is where copyright exposure lives, where privacy obligations attach, where bias originates, and where the EU AI Act Article 10 (data and data governance) obligations bite. It is also the component vendors and foundation model providers most often refuse to disclose — see vendor-component opacity in Anti-Patterns (§6.2).

**Required disclosure fields per dataset**:
- Source (internal collection, licensed third-party, public/open dataset, web-scraped, customer-derived, synthetic)
- License or contractual basis for use (CC-BY, custom commercial license, internal-only, customer consent, etc.)
- Sampling decisions (what was included, what was excluded, what filtering applied)
- PII / PHI / sensitive-attribute presence (and redaction or pseudonymization applied)
- Geographic origin (relevant for GDPR data-residency analysis)
- Date range of collection (relevant for legitimate-interest analysis under GDPR)
- For customer-derived data: consent basis and contractual provision authorizing training use

**Owner**: `data-governance` for sourcing legitimacy and license posture; `privacy-counsel` for PII / GDPR / CCPA analysis; `ip-counsel` for copyright analysis on web-scraped corpora; `security-architect` for data-in-transit and data-at-rest protection during training.

**Evidentiary use**: Article 10 + Annex IV §2(c) (datasets, their provenance, scope and main characteristics). GDPR Article 22 automated-decision-making coverage. Copyright litigation (NYT v. OpenAI category). FCRA "accuracy of consumer report" analysis under § 1681e(b) if the consumer-report theory holds.

### 2.3 Evaluation Data — `[Owner: 🛡️]`

**What is inventoried**: Test sets, holdout sets, benchmark suites, subgroup-disaggregated evaluation datasets, red-team adversarial prompts, and any data used to measure model performance at any point in the development or deployment lifecycle.

**Why it matters**: Evaluation data is what defends the claim "this system performs as documented." EU AI Act Article 15 (accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity) requires subgroup-disaggregated evaluation for high-risk systems — meaning evaluation broken down by protected characteristics where they are relevant to the system's purpose. NYC AEDT bias audits require evaluation evidence under Local Law 144's audit-publication regime. NIST AI 600-1 MEASURE function calls for evaluation data documentation as a baseline practice.

**Required disclosure fields per evaluation dataset**:
- Holdout discipline (was it strictly held out from training? if not, what contamination risk exists?)
- Subgroup composition (race, gender, age, geographic distribution — where lawfully collected)
- Provenance (collected for evaluation purposes vs repurposed from training)
- Metrics computed (accuracy, F1, calibration, demographic parity, equal-opportunity, etc.)
- Date of last evaluation run (relevant for "is the production model still meeting evaluation thresholds?" analysis)

**Owner**: `ai-architect` + `data-architect` for evaluation design and holdout discipline; `compliance-officer` for bias-audit evidence; `experimentation-analyst` for metric computation.

**Evidentiary use**: Article 15 + Annex IV §3 (performance metrics, accuracy, robustness). NYC AEDT bias audit publication. State AG investigations on disparate-impact claims.

### 2.4 Prompts — `[Owner: 💻]`

**What is inventoried**: Every system prompt, prompt template, persona definition, role assignment, and instruction injected into a model invocation at runtime. Includes version-controlled prompt history with diff-tracking, A/B-tested prompt variants, and per-customer or per-tenant prompt customizations.

**Why it matters**: The prompt is where behavior is configured. A model with one prompt may meet EU AI Act Article 14 (human oversight) requirements; the same model with a different prompt may not. A change to a system prompt without re-evaluation is a behavior change without evidence — which is an Article 15 + Annex IV §4 evidentiary gap. The prompt is also where attempts to bypass safety controls (jailbreaks, prompt injection) leave the most traceable fingerprint.

**Required disclosure fields per prompt**:
- Prompt identifier and version
- Diff history (when did the prompt change, who changed it, what changed)
- Variables interpolated at runtime (user input, retrieved context, tool-use scaffolding)
- A/B variants and traffic allocation
- Evaluation evidence for the current production prompt (link to §2.3 evaluation runs)

**Owner**: `ai-architect` for prompt architecture; `cio` + `enterprise-systems` for version-control discipline; `security-architect` for prompt-injection defense.

**Evidentiary use**: Article 14 + Annex IV §3 (human oversight measures). Incident-response forensics when behavior drifts. Customer disputes over output quality.

### 2.5 Retrieval Corpora — `[Owner: 🛡️]`

**What is inventoried**: Every document corpus, knowledge base, vector index, embedded knowledge graph, and document store from which the system retrieves information at runtime to ground model outputs. Includes RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) corpora, agentic-system reference databases, and any "knowledge cutoff extension" mechanism.

**Why it matters**: Retrieval corpora are where **PII leakage at runtime** most often originates. An indexed customer-support corpus, an embedded HR-policy document store, an internal-wiki vector index — each carries the risk that documents the corpus author never intended to surface to a particular user's prompt now do so. EU AI Act Article 10 (data and data governance) obligations attach to retrieval corpora the same way they attach to training data. GDPR Article 5(1)(f) integrity-and-confidentiality obligations attach to the access-control posture of the corpus.

**Required disclosure fields per corpus**:
- Source documents indexed (with provenance per document, where lawfully obtainable)
- Refresh cadence (real-time, daily, weekly, ad-hoc, frozen)
- Access controls (which user roles can trigger retrieval against this corpus, which cannot)
- PII / PHI / sensitive-attribute presence audit
- Indexed-document deletion policy (when a source document is deleted, when is it removed from the index)
- Embedding model used (cross-references §2.1 — embedding models are models)

**Owner**: `data-governance` for corpus sourcing and access control posture; `security-architect` for retrieval boundary enforcement; `privacy-counsel` for PII-in-corpus risk; `cio` for refresh-cadence operations.

**Evidentiary use**: GDPR + CCPA data-subject access requests reaching into retrieval corpora. Article 10 + Annex IV §2(g) (data input, training, validation, testing). Incident response when an unauthorized document surfaces in a response.

### 2.6 Third-Party Components — `[Owner: 💻]`

**What is inventoried**: Every external API, MCP server, third-party model endpoint, third-party agent-tool, embedded SaaS classifier, and external service the system invokes at runtime to extend its capability. This is the row most likely to carry vendor-component opacity — see Anti-Patterns (§6.2) — because third-party providers often refuse to disclose their training data, evaluation, or system prompts.

**Why it matters**: Every third-party component is a privileged integration surface and a governance gap. The product organization inherits the security posture, the training-data posture, the bias posture, and the evidentiary posture of every third-party component it integrates. Q2-3.2 `mcp-governance.md` documents the MCP-specific governance pattern; this pack extends the same discipline to all third-party AI components.

**Required disclosure fields per third-party component**:
- Provider identity and contractual relationship (SaaS contract, OSS dependency, partner agreement)
- API endpoint or MCP server reference
- Capability invoked (text generation, classification, retrieval, code execution, web access)
- Data flow (what flows out to the provider, what flows back, what is logged on the provider side)
- Provider's published documentation maturity (do they publish a model card, an SBOM, an AI BOM of their own?)
- Vendor-risk assessment status (SOC 2 evidence, ISO 27001 evidence, AI-specific assessment if performed)
- Fallback behavior when the third-party component is unavailable or degraded

**Owner**: `cio` for vendor relationships and contractual posture; `security-architect` for data-flow governance; `compliance-officer` for vendor-risk assessment; `chief-architect` for fallback and degradation architecture.

**Evidentiary use**: EU AI Act provider/deployer distinction analysis. FCRA "furnisher of information" obligations under § 1681s-2. Vendor-breach incident response.

### 2.7 Fine-Tuning Recipes — `[Owner: 🛡️]`

**What is inventoried**: For every fine-tuned or instruct-tuned model in §2.1, the complete recipe used to produce it. Includes base model, training data references (cross-references §2.2), hyperparameters, compute used, evaluation gates passed, and the operator who ran the recipe.

**Why it matters**: A fine-tuning recipe is the difference between a reproducible AI system and a one-off artifact whose behavior cannot be defended evidentially. EU AI Act Annex IV §2(b) ("the design specifications of the system, namely the general logic of the AI system and of the algorithms") requires reproducibility-grade documentation. FCRA "accuracy and procedures" analysis under § 1681e(b) requires evidence of the methodology used to produce a scoring component. Without a recipe, post-hoc explanation of behavior is reconstructive guessing.

**Required disclosure fields per recipe**:
- Base model identifier and version
- Training data references (point to §2.2 entries)
- Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size, epochs, optimization algorithm, LoRA / QLoRA / full-fine-tune configuration)
- Compute environment (cloud provider, instance type, GPU hours, cost)
- Evaluation gates required to pass before promotion (link to §2.3 evaluation runs)
- Operator and approval chain (who ran the recipe, who approved promotion to production)
- Reproducibility checkpoint (can the recipe be re-run today and produce a functionally equivalent model?)

**Owner**: `ai-architect` for recipe authorship; `ml-engineer` for hyperparameter and compute discipline; `compliance-officer` for evaluation-gate evidence; `security-architect` for operator-and-approval audit trail.

**Evidentiary use**: Article 9 + Annex IV §2 (risk management system, design specifications). FCRA accuracy-and-procedures evidence. IP licensing analysis if the fine-tuned model is to be shared, sold, or open-sourced.

### 2.8 Deployment Artifacts — `[Owner: 💻]`

**What is inventoried**: The runtime-equivalent of "the binary that ships" — versioned model weights (where self-hosted), inference infrastructure, autoscaling configuration, monitoring configuration, alert thresholds, and rollback procedures. The bridge from "what we built" to "what is running in production right now."

**Why it matters**: The other seven components describe what the system *is*; deployment artifacts describe what is actually *running*. EU AI Act Article 15 + Annex IV §3 (monitoring, functioning, control) cannot be satisfied without a complete inventory of the production runtime. NIST AI 600-1 MEASURE function calls for runtime documentation. Incident response is impossible without it.

**Required disclosure fields per deployment artifact**:
- Versioned model weights identifier (for self-hosted; SHA or registry reference)
- Inference infrastructure (provider, region, instance configuration, GPU type)
- Autoscaling configuration (min/max replicas, scaling triggers)
- Monitoring configuration (which metrics are tracked, which are alerted on)
- Alert thresholds (latency, error rate, output-distribution drift, content-safety violations)
- Rollback procedure (how does production revert to a known-good state)
- Change log (every promotion, rollback, configuration change with operator and timestamp)

**Owner**: `cio` + `enterprise-systems` for runtime operations; `security-architect` for production-access controls; `chief-architect` for autoscaling and degradation design; `ai-architect` for model-promotion discipline.

**Evidentiary use**: Article 15 + Annex IV §3 (monitoring, functioning, control). Incident response forensics. State AG investigations establishing what was running when an alleged harm occurred.

## 3. AI BOM as Evidentiary Upstream `[Owner: 🛡️]`

The AI BOM is the **evidentiary upstream** that downstream regulatory documentation rests on. Three regulatory paths illustrate why regulators reach for the BOM first.

### 3.1 EU AI Act Article 11 + Annex IV path

Article 11 establishes the obligation to maintain technical documentation for high-risk AI systems. Annex IV §§1-9 specifies the 9 documentation elements. Q2-3.4 `eu-ai-act-annex-iv.md` documents the Annex IV taxonomy in operational detail. The AI BOM feeds Annex IV directly:
- Annex IV §1 (general description) ← BOM §2.1 (Models) + §2.8 (Deployment Artifacts)
- Annex IV §2 (design specifications, datasets, data provenance) ← BOM §2.2 (Training Data) + §2.3 (Evaluation Data) + §2.5 (Retrieval Corpora) + §2.7 (Fine-Tuning Recipes)
- Annex IV §3 (monitoring, functioning, control) ← BOM §2.4 (Prompts) + §2.6 (Third-Party Components) + §2.8 (Deployment Artifacts)
- Annex IV §4-9 (testing, performance, risk management, compliance with harmonised standards, instructions of use, post-market monitoring) ← all 8 BOM components feed in

A high-risk AI system cannot satisfy Article 11 without an AI BOM. A high-risk AI system **with** an AI BOM still requires the structural Annex IV documentation drafted on top — the BOM is necessary but not sufficient.

### 3.2 FCRA adverse-action path

If the Eightfold class action theory holds at any stage (the claim that AI candidate-scoring outputs may qualify as "consumer reports" under 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(d), with the AI vendor potentially qualifying as a "consumer reporting agency" under § 1681a(f)), the AI BOM is the artifact that establishes the threshold question — does the in-scope candidate-scoring component exist, and what is it. The four FCRA procedural obligations enumerated in `sensitive-skill-guardrails.md` §1.4 then attach to any adverse action the employer takes in reliance on the output:
- 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3) pre-adverse-action notice
- 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3) copy-of-report delivery
- 15 U.S.C. § 1681g summary-of-rights
- 15 U.S.C. § 1681i 30-day dispute window + reinvestigation

The BOM does not satisfy these obligations. The BOM is what makes the obligations **visible and assessable**. Counsel determines whether the statutory definitions apply and whether the obligations are triggered. See `## Cannot Assess Without Licensed Counsel` below.

### 3.3 State Attorney General investigation path

Texas TRAIGA, NYC AEDT, California ICRAA + CCRAA, Illinois AI Video Interview Act, Colorado AI Act — each empowers a state Attorney General (or in NYC's case, the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection) to investigate AI systems used in consequential decisions. The investigation reaches for the BOM first, because the BOM establishes:
- Whether the system is in scope (does it produce automated decisions? does it score candidates?)
- Whether the model, data, and evaluation evidence satisfy bias-audit obligations
- Whether the disclosure obligations attached to the BOM contents have been met

Q2-1.C `state-ai-hiring-laws.md` documents the regime-specific obligations. The BOM is the artifact those obligations attach to.

## 4. V2V Phase 3 (Commitments) Integration `[Owner: 💻]`

The AI BOM is **not optional** for a product organization shipping an AI system. It is part of the Phase 3 (Commitments) Definition of Done, on the same gating tier as security review, accessibility audit, and launch readiness.

### Phase 3 (Commitments) gate — pre-deployment

Before an AI system promotes from Phase 2 (Discovery) to Phase 3 (Commitments) GO, the AI BOM must be:
- **Complete** — all 8 components populated for the in-scope AI system
- **Owned** — each component's "Owner" field assigned to a named agent (per §2 above)
- **Versioned** — under version control with diff history (treat the BOM as code, not as a document)
- **Cross-referenced** — links from the BOM to upstream sources (training data licenses, model provider contracts, vendor risk assessments)
- **Counsel-reviewed where the `## Cannot Assess Without` section applies** — see §5 below

### Phase 4 (Execution) carry-forward — runtime audit trail

The BOM becomes a live operational artifact during Phase 4 (Execution). Every change to any of the 8 components — a model version bump, a prompt revision, a new corpus indexed, a new third-party component integrated, a recipe re-run — produces a diff in the BOM. The BOM is the audit trail. A change to a production component without a corresponding BOM update is an Article 11 evidentiary gap, an Article 15 monitoring gap, and an incident-response handicap.

### Phase 5 (Value Realization) carry-forward — adverse-action and inspection substrate

The BOM is the evidentiary substrate that regulators, plaintiff counsel, and state Attorneys General reach for during Phase 5 (Value Realization) — when the system has been in production long enough for an alleged harm to materialize. A complete, versioned, owned BOM is the difference between defending a Phase 5 inspection on documented evidence and reconstructing post-hoc what the system was doing six months ago.

## 5. Sensitive-Adjacent Posture (M41) `[Owner: 🛡️]`

This pack is **sensitive-adjacent** per M41. The AI BOM artifact itself is operational documentation — produced, owned, and operated by the product organization. The scaffolding above (8 components, fields per component, V2V phase integration) is operationally useful regardless of whether legal review attaches.

The **use** of the BOM in adverse-action proceedings, regulatory inspections, conformity assessments, or state Attorney General investigations crosses into territory where legal review is non-substitutable. The pack treats this distinction structurally:

- **In the body** (§1-§4): operational guidance a product organization can act on. ROI framing is "drafting and triage" — the pack helps a team produce a structured first-pass BOM that a human expert then reviews, validates, and owns.
- **In the Reviewer Checklist** (§5.1 below): explicit sign-off items the human reviewer must complete before the BOM is treated as evidence.
- **In Cannot Assess Without** (§5.2 below): the explicit list of items the pack deliberately does not opine on, where counsel review is required before the BOM contents are acted upon in any regulatory or adverse-action context.

Per `roi-display.md` Sensitive Skill ROI Framing section: this pack and any skill that consumes it MUST frame ROI as "drafting and triage" — NEVER as "time saved on legal review" or "faster compliance review." The pack produces a structured first pass; the reviewer performs review.

### 5.1 Reviewer Checklist

For any AI BOM produced from this pack, the human reviewer signs off on:

- [ ] **Component completeness**: All 8 BOM component categories populated for each in-scope AI system. No "TBD" or "see vendor" placeholders in deployed-system BOMs.
- [ ] **Owner assignment**: Each component has a named owner from the registry. "Owner: AI team" is insufficient; name the agent.
- [ ] **Vendor-component opacity flagged**: Where third-party providers (§2.6) refuse to disclose training data, evaluation, or system prompts, the gap is recorded explicitly in the BOM. Do not paper over with provider marketing claims.
- [ ] **Retrieval-corpus access controls verified**: For each corpus in §2.5, the access-control posture has been independently verified by `security-architect`, not just declared.
- [ ] **PII / PHI / sensitive-attribute audit complete**: For training data (§2.2), evaluation data (§2.3), and retrieval corpora (§2.5), the sensitive-attribute presence audit is current (not older than 90 days for high-risk systems).
- [ ] **Version-control discipline verified**: The BOM lives in version control with diff history accessible. A point-in-time PDF export is not version control.
- [ ] **Cross-references to upstream sources resolved**: Links from the BOM to training data licenses, model provider contracts, vendor risk assessments, and evaluation runs all dereference to current documents.
- [ ] **Phase 3 (Commitments) gate signed off**: A named approver from `cio` + `ai-architect` + `compliance-officer` has signed off on the BOM as part of pre-deployment Definition of Done.
- [ ] **Counsel engaged on items in `## Cannot Assess Without`**: For every item in §5.2 below that applies to the in-scope system, counsel review has been initiated or completed.

### 5.2 Cannot Assess Without Licensed Counsel

The following determinations cannot be made from this pack alone. Each item requires counsel review in the relevant jurisdiction before the BOM contents are acted upon in any regulatory, adverse-action, or disclosure context.

- **Whether the AI BOM's enumerated components qualify as a "consumer report" under FCRA 15 U.S.C. § 1681a(d)** (cross-reference `sensitive-skill-guardrails.md` §1.4 + Eightfold AI class action filed 2026-01-20; theory is novel and at pleading stage but treated as live exposure)
- **Whether the producing entity qualifies as a "consumer reporting agency" under FCRA § 1681a(f)** (novel theory; counsel determination)
- **FCRA pre-adverse-action notice obligations under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)** — whether the employer-user is obligated to provide consumer with notice plus copy of report before acting on BOM-enumerated scoring component output
- **FCRA copy-of-report delivery under 15 U.S.C. § 1681b(b)(3)** — whether the actual report (not summary, not redacted) must be delivered
- **FCRA summary-of-rights delivery under 15 U.S.C. § 1681g** — whether the CFPB-prescribed summary attaches
- **FCRA 30-day dispute window + reinvestigation under 15 U.S.C. § 1681i** — whether the statutory 30-day dispute period and reinvestigation obligations attach to the BOM-enumerated scoring component
- **State-law layered obligations on candidate-scoring components**: California ICRAA / CCRAA, NY layered statutes, MA, IL (AI Video Interview Act), MN, OK, WA, CO (Colorado AI Act effective 2026), TX (TRAIGA) — cross-reference Q2-1.C `state-ai-hiring-laws.md`
- **NYC AEDT bias-audit publication requirements** under Local Law 144 — cross-reference Q2-1.C
- **EU AI Act high-risk classification** under Article 6 + Annex III — cross-reference Q2-1.A `ai-act-readiness.md` (the BOM does not determine high-risk status; the BOM is required IF the system is high-risk per the Article 6 + Annex III determination upstream)
- **EU AI Act Annex IV technical documentation sufficiency** — whether the BOM contents satisfy Annex IV §§1-9 for a specific high-risk system — cross-reference Q2-3.4 `eu-ai-act-annex-iv.md`
- **EU AI Act conformity-assessment route selection** under Article 43 + Annex VI vs Annex VII — counsel + notified-body determination
- **Title VII disparate-impact exposure** on the underlying scoring model — parallel to and independent of FCRA per `sensitive-skill-guardrails.md` §1.4; EEOC + DOJ guidance on AI in employment decisions applies
- **EEOC technical-assistance guidance compliance** for AI hiring tools — counsel + HR-policy determination
- **UK Data Protection Act 2018 + UK GDPR Article 22 automated-decision-making coverage** for UK-deployed systems — UK is not bound by EU AI Act; separate counsel determination required
- **GDPR Article 22 automated-decision-making rights** for EU-deployed systems — including the right to obtain human review of automated decisions
- **GDPR Article 35 Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) obligations** when training data or retrieval corpora include personal data at scale
- **IP licensing analysis** on training data (§2.2) — particularly for web-scraped corpora (NYT v. OpenAI category exposure) and customer-derived data (whether contractual provisions actually authorize training use)
- **Copyright analysis on fine-tuned model outputs** — whether the fine-tuned model in §2.1 produces outputs that may infringe on rightsholders represented in the training data
- **Trade-secret protection posture on the BOM itself** — when, where, and how the BOM may be disclosed to regulators, plaintiffs, customers, partners, or the public without compromising trade-secret status of training data, evaluation methodology, or prompts
- **Cross-border data transfer obligations** under GDPR Chapter V (SCCs, adequacy decisions, Schrems II analysis) when training data, evaluation data, or retrieval corpora cross jurisdictions
- **Sector-specific overlays** (HIPAA for healthcare AI, GLBA for financial-services AI, FERPA for education AI, COPPA for child-directed AI) — counsel + sector-regulator determination

## 6. Anti-Patterns / Common Failures `[Owner: 🛡️]`

Product organizations producing AI BOMs through 2025-2026 have surfaced a consistent set of failure modes. Each is recoverable, but each undermines the BOM's evidentiary value if left in place.

### 6.1 BOM-as-checkbox

The BOM is produced once, signed off at a Phase 3 (Commitments) gate, filed in a compliance directory, and never updated. By the time a regulator or plaintiff counsel asks for it, the production system has drifted from the BOM in every component — different model versions, different prompts, new retrieval corpora indexed, new third-party components integrated. The BOM is no longer evidence; it is a historical artifact of what the system was at one point. **Recovery**: treat the BOM as code — version control, diff history, change reviews, CI gates that fail builds if the BOM is not updated alongside production component changes.

### 6.2 Vendor-component opacity

A third-party AI component (§2.6) is integrated, but the provider refuses to disclose training data, evaluation methodology, or system prompts. The BOM author either omits the third-party component (creating a Phase 3 gap), papers over the gap with provider marketing claims ("provider attests to bias-free training data"), or treats the provider's published model card as sufficient documentation. **Recovery**: record the gap explicitly. The BOM should say "provider declines to disclose training data composition; vendor-risk assessment notes this gap; the in-scope system inherits unknown training-data posture from this component." Counsel and `compliance-officer` then determine whether the gap is acceptable for the system's risk classification.

### 6.3 Retrieval-corpus leak risk

An indexed retrieval corpus (§2.5) contains PII, PHI, sensitive attributes, or proprietary internal documents that the corpus author did not intend to surface to user prompts. The corpus is indexed once, access controls are declared but not independently verified, and the PII audit is either skipped or stale. At runtime, a user prompt retrieves a document the user should not have access to. **Recovery**: the Reviewer Checklist item "Retrieval-corpus access controls verified" is non-negotiable. `security-architect` independently verifies, not just `data-governance` self-declares. PII audits run on a cadence (90 days for high-risk systems), with the date of the most recent audit recorded in the BOM.

### 6.4 Training-data lineage gaps

A fine-tuned model in §2.1 was produced six months ago. The recipe (§2.7) was either never recorded or has been lost. The training dataset references in §2.2 point to corpora that have since been deleted or refreshed beyond recognition. Reproducibility is impossible; post-hoc explanation of the model's behavior is reconstructive guessing. **Recovery**: every recipe is version-controlled at the moment it runs, not reconstructed later. Training data corpora are snapshot-pinned at recipe execution time. If lineage has already been lost, the BOM records the gap explicitly — "training data lineage incomplete; reproducibility limited" — and counsel determines whether the gap blocks deployment.

### 6.5 BOM-without-versioning

The BOM lives as a PDF in a SharePoint folder, an HTML page in a static-site repo, or a wiki entry. There is no diff history, no change review, no operator attribution per change. Every change overwrites the previous version. **Recovery**: the BOM lives in version control (Git, equivalent). Changes go through pull request or equivalent change-review process with named approvers from `cio` + `ai-architect` + `compliance-officer`. Point-in-time exports (PDF, HTML) are derived artifacts, not the source of truth.

### 6.6 BOM-without-counsel-engagement-on-§5.2-items

The BOM is complete in §2-§4 and signed off at the Phase 3 gate, but no item in `## Cannot Assess Without Licensed Counsel` (§5.2) has been routed to counsel. The product organization treats the BOM as self-sufficient documentation. At Phase 5 inspection, counsel engages for the first time under time pressure and discovers obligations that should have been resolved months earlier. **Recovery**: the Reviewer Checklist item "Counsel engaged on items in Cannot Assess Without" is a Phase 3 gate-blocking sign-off. Counsel review is initiated at Phase 3, not deferred to Phase 5.

## 8. Operating Principle

> *An AI BOM is not paperwork; it is the evidentiary substrate that downstream regulatory documentation rests on. A product organization that treats it as a Phase 3 (Commitments) drafting-and-triage discipline produces evidence at deployment. A product organization that treats it as a Phase 5 (Value Realization) afterthought produces a reconstruction.*
